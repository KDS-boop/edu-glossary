Decentralization is a design principle in which authority and decision-making are distributed across all network participants rather than concentrated in a single entity. In the context of blockchain, this means no single party can unilaterally modify data, block transactions, or change the rules of the network.

Measuring how decentralized a network actually is remains challenging. One practical metric is the **Nakamoto coefficient** — the minimum number of entities that would need to collude to compromise the system. If five mining pools control over 50% of a network's hash rate, the Nakamoto coefficient for that network is five. A higher number indicates greater practical decentralization.

## Levels of Decentralization

Decentralization exists on a spectrum. **Pseudo-decentralization** appears decentralized but certain actors hold disproportionate influence — for example, a few mining pools controlling the majority of Bitcoin's hash rate. **True decentralization** is achieved when a sufficiently large number of nodes are geographically distributed and each holds relatively equal influence. **Over-decentralization** can lead to coordination problems and slow decision-making.

## Key Benefits

Decentralized systems are more resistant to censorship and single points of failure — there is no single server or authority that can be taken down to bring the entire system down. Censorship resistance ensures that everyone can participate without fear of being blocked. Data transparency also improves because all participants hold an identical copy of the ledger.

## Challenges and Governance

Decentralization brings its own challenges: how do you make decisions when there are disagreements among thousands of participants? Governance mechanisms such as improvement proposals (BIP, EIP), DAO voting, and on-chain governance are attempts to address these coordination problems without returning control to a single center.
